02|The Big Three

NVIDIA reinstates Jensen Huang, and its stock jumps about a third in morning trading

What it is: NVIDIA's board voted unanimously Monday to reinstate Jensen Huang as president, chief executive, and director, effective immediately, 11 days after he resigned. The board said it received "input from senior levels of the United States government" and is confident his return "poses no national security risk"; Colette Kress returns to chief financial officer only; an independent committee's investigation continues, a National Security and Export Control Committee oversees trade compliance, and a Chief Compliance Officer search began (NVIDIA release). Shares rose as much as 33 percent to $167.20 from Friday's $125.53, adding close to $1 trillion to a value near $4.02 trillion, still about 31 percent below the Oct 6 record; the S&P 500 rose about 4.5 percent and the Nasdaq about 6.5 percent (CNBC; NDTV Profit citing the Associated Press). Open items remain: Huang's closed-door House interviews on Dec 1, and a Reuters report, from two unnamed sources, that the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) is examining NVIDIA's accounting. Separately, Arm said its chief executive Rene Haas signed a new agreement through 2033 and becomes SoftBank Group vice chairman on Dec 1, after NVIDIA's search firm had approached him (Arm newsroom; CNBC).

Why it matters: Investors treated last week's fall as a governance and financing scare, not a demand problem, and the speed of the reversal shows how much of the AI trade hangs on one company's chief executive. Washington's role in the decision makes chip leadership a national security matter, which keeps Congress and the SEC in the story.

Control check: If the week's 23 percent NVIDIA fall was about leadership and not demand, the rest of the chain should snap back with it, and most did: CoreWeave rose as much as 31 percent, Micron nearly 17 percent, and Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing as much as 21 percent in Monday morning trading (CNBC; NDTV Profit), but Anthropic, the one pure lab, stayed near $89, roughly flat on Friday's close (Yahoo Finance), so the rebound was about chips and not about labs (Likely).

Server makers ask for up to 45 percent more on signed AI contracts, and memory makers keep the gain

What it is: The Financial Times reported Sunday that Dell, HPE, Lenovo, and Supermicro are demanding 25 to 45 percent more at delivery for NVIDIA-based servers than their contracts say; servers built on graphics processing units (GPUs) are up at least 20 to 30 percent and memory-heavy nodes built on central processing units (CPUs) have more than tripled in price, and trading firms such as Jane Street, Optiver, and Hudson River Trading are among those paying (TrendForce, Tom's Hardware, citing the FT). XTX sued Dell in an Irish court in June over a $549 million order after Dell asked for $62 million to $70 million more. JPMorgan's Nov 20 note says the three big memory makers can meet only 60 to 70 percent of 2027 DRAM demand even as supply grows 20 to 25 percent; HPE now writes quotes that allow repricing (TrendForce). Dell reports Tuesday with an AI backlog of $95 billion as of July 31 (Yahoo Finance).

Why it matters: Demand for computing power outruns supply even in a selloff, and the extra dollars flow to memory makers while the bill lands on buyers that borrow to build. CoreWeave's third-quarter interest expense of $846 million was more than four times its $207 million operating income (Yahoo Finance), so the buyers with the thinnest margins feel it first.

Control check: If memory is why server prices are rising up to 45 percent, memory makers should be sold out, and they are: JPMorgan says Samsung, SK hynix, and Kioxia can meet only 60 to 70 percent of 2027 DRAM demand even with 20 to 25 percent more output (JPMorgan note, Nov 20), while SDxCentral reports SK hynix moved its relief estimate from 2028 to 2030; the missing evidence is Dell's own margin and backlog, due Tuesday (Likely).

Anthropic closes at $88.91, below its debut, while its filing shows $900.9 billion of spending commitments

What it is: Anthropic, trading as ANT after its initial public offering (IPO), closed Friday at $88.91, down 8.5 percent from Thursday's $97.17 and not far from the $83 offer price, on 399.6 million shares, and traded near $89 on Monday morning (Yahoo Finance; Reuters). Its prospectus reports annualized run-rate revenue of about $81 billion at Oct 31, up from about $71 billion at Sept 30, and about $900.9 billion of committed spending, nearly all for computing: about $451.6 billion before 2030 and about $70.7 billion within 12 months (Anthropic prospectus, as summarized by NDTV Profit). Adjusted operating income was $4.08 billion for nine months, but under standard accounting the company lost $1.26 billion from operations (CNBC). In a podcast clip, White House adviser David Sacks said Anthropic is marketing recursive self-improvement, meaning each Claude version helping build the next, in its roadshow and prospectus; the S-1 defines the term that way.

Why it matters: The market will pay for the lab but is not yet sure growth covers the bill: $70.7 billion due within a year equals about 87 percent of the current $81 billion run-rate revenue, by our arithmetic. Run-rate means this month's pace stretched to a year, not cash in the bank.

Control check: If $900.9 billion of spending commitments are real, suppliers and landlords should show matching contracts, and the prospectus names one, SpaceX at up to $120.5 billion through 2029 (CNBC), but no supplier has yet reported Anthropic revenue and Anthropic's own first quarterly report is not out, so the cash side is unverified (Guessing).

Questions worth asking

Three testable questions from today's news, each with the evidence that would answer it.

1. When a server maker reprices a signed contract by 45 percent, who really carries the cost: the buyer, the buyer's customers, or its lenders?

What would answer it: Dell's results Tuesday (AI server revenue against its $19 billion guide, backlog near $95 billion, margin above its 13.4 percent guide), CoreWeave's next loan terms against its interest bill, and the court record in XTX's Irish lawsuit against Dell over a $549 million contract.

2. If Anthropic must pay $70.7 billion over the next 12 months against about $81 billion of annualized revenue, where else should the strain show first: supplier backlogs, power contracts, or lenders' filings?

What would answer it: Anthropic's first quarterly report (cash from operations against the $70.7 billion due), supplier filings that name Anthropic, and any trimmed or delayed capacity deals with SpaceX, Nscale, or Lambda.

3. If a model that fits in about 20 gigabytes of memory now leads open models under 100 billion parameters at coding, how much paid cloud use moves onto laptops, and at what battery and electricity cost?

What would answer it: An independent rerun of the DeepSWE and Vals Index scores, measured watts and minutes per task on laptops, and whether open-model usage on OpenRouter shifts toward small local models.

03|Use Cases
What superintelligence is actually doing in the world, and how well it works.

Swindon social workers cut paperwork time 63 percent with an AI note tool

Who and what: Swindon Borough Council's adult social care team ran a pilot of an AI tool that supports social workers with transcribing and summarizing case notes and conversations. The Department of Health and Social Care and the Department for Education cited it on Nov 20 in the first national guidance on AI in children's and adult social care. Tool name, model, chips, and cloud: not disclosed. A second council, Peterborough, built an assistant called Hey Geraldine from an occupational therapist's knowledge.

Measured result: Administrative time fell 63 percent, a Care Act assessment conversation fell from 1.5 hours to 30 minutes, and writing up an assessment fell from 4 hours to 1.5 hours; Hey Geraldine answered over 1,200 questions and saved about 15 minutes each. Tag: Likely (government release of Nov 20; no method or audit published).

Strength and weakness: Strength: staff with visual impairments or with English as a second language benefited most, and the time went back to care. Weakness: pilot figures from the sponsor, no error rate or complaint data reported, and the guidance itself says professional judgment must stay central.

Cost and time to deploy: Not disclosed; pilot duration and licensing cost not published.

Maturity: Pilot

An AI phone agent resolves 59 percent of Candid Health's insurance denial calls

Who and what: Candid Health, a billing platform for large healthcare organizations, uses ElevenLabs' ElevenAgents to phone insurers about denied claims and check patient eligibility, so human billers can spend time on harder work. Staff in customer operations, not engineers, built and tune the agents, and Candid can bring its own language models. Model, chips, and cloud: not disclosed. Published Nov 19, one day before this window opened.

Measured result: Across 12,860 calls the agents saved more than 850 hours of staff time per month and resolved 59 percent, where resolved means the reason for the denial and the next step were both found; the average call lasted 8 minutes 46 seconds. Tag: Likely (vendor case study; no outside audit).

Strength and weakness: Strength: non-engineers can build and fix agents, and calls surfaced upstream errors such as a provider not credentialed on the service date. Weakness: 41 percent of calls were not resolved, the agents investigate but do not appeal, and the numbers come from the vendor and customer.

Cost and time to deploy: Not disclosed; Candid's engineers connected telephony, interfaces, and call reports within 48 hours.

Maturity: Production

04|Known knowns
Confirmed news this window. Each card has the bull case, the bear case, my call, and who wins and loses.

Cards 1 to 3 are the debate behind The Big Three; cards 4 and 5 are the other stories worth a full read on a Monday.

1. NVIDIA reinstates Jensen Huang, and its stock jumps about a third in morning trading: bull, bear, and my call

+Bull

A founder who runs the supply chain, the customer financing, and the product roadmap is back, nearly $1 trillion of value returned in a morning, and the neoclouds that depend on NVIDIA's guarantees rallied with it. The government's input removes the immediate risk of a forced outside chief executive.

−Bear

The board said six days earlier it would not reinstate him, the independent investigation and an SEC accounting inquiry are still open, and Huang faces closed-door House interviews Dec 1 about whether a relationship was meant to help China. A single decision by a small board moved the whole AI trade, and that fragility has not gone away.

My call (Certain on the release and prices (NVIDIA; CNBC); Likely on the SEC inquiry (Reuters, two unnamed sources))
The rebound holds through Dell's results and fades only if Congress or the SEC produces a new fact (Guessing). The deeper lesson is that investors priced governance, not demand.
Who wins
NVIDIA and its shareholders, CoreWeave and other borrowers tied to NVIDIA's guarantees, memory makers, and Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing.
Who loses
Short sellers, rivals hoping for a distracted NVIDIA, and the board's credibility.

2. Server makers ask for up to 45 percent more on signed AI contracts, and memory makers keep the gain: bull, bear, and my call

+Bull

Pricing power this strong confirms demand: financial firms told the FT they have never said no because the compute is valuable, and Dell's gross margin was 21.0 percent in its July quarter. Memory makers collect most of the new money and keep expanding output.

−Bear

A buyer like XTX is already suing, debt-heavy neoclouds have the least room to absorb cost, and a price rise that only passes along memory costs does not make servers much more profitable for the sellers (Yahoo Finance). If buyers push back or delay deliveries, revenue timing slips.

My call (Likely: single source (Financial Times) with several outlets repeating it; Dell has not commented in the sources read)
Dell's AI revenue lands at or above its $19 billion guide, and the repricing spreads to more vendors in December (Likely). Watch whether neoclouds pass costs to their own customers.
Who wins
Memory makers (Samsung, SK hynix, Micron), server makers with pricing power, and buyers with cash.
Who loses
Debt-financed neoclouds, smaller buyers, and anyone whose contract lacks a fixed price.

3. Anthropic closes at $88.91, below its debut, while its filing shows $900.9 billion of spending commitments: bull, bear, and my call

+Bull

Revenue is compounding fast: run-rate rose about $10 billion in one month, nine-month revenue growth was 1,206 percent, and the company reports adjusted operating profit of $4.08 billion (CNBC). Its roadshow targets computing at 45 to 50 percent of revenue and a long-term adjusted operating margin near 35 percent.

−Bear

Standard accounting shows a $1.26 billion operating loss, and a net loss above $104 billion through September mostly reflects a $94 billion noncash charge tied to securities issued to Google and Amazon (CNBC). Cash due within 12 months, $70.7 billion, is about 87 percent of run-rate revenue, and the stock already sits near $83.

My call (Certain on filing figures (Anthropic prospectus; CNBC); Guessing on whether growth covers them)
ANT trades within 10 percent of $83 until its first quarterly report (Guessing). The commitments are real, but they are paid over years and mostly to suppliers that also invested in Anthropic.
Who wins
Suppliers with contracts (SpaceX, Google, Amazon, Microsoft) and early holders who sold at the offer.
Who loses
New buyers near the highs, and neoclouds if Anthropic trims conditional deals.

4. Trump and Xi agree on "joint guardrails" with no text, while chip rules stay split.

After the Shenzhen meeting Trump said he and Xi agreed to "joint guardrails" on superintelligence and threatened a "tremendous tax" on US firms building data centers abroad, while Bessent said the H200 remains the most advanced chip China may buy.

+Bull

Both sides said humans should stay in charge of military AI decisions (Reuters, citing Bessent), and a stated ceiling on chip sales gives buyers a rule to plan around. Commerce also closed remote-access routes to restricted users.

−Bear

No document, scope, or channel was published; the White House issued no readout and only China's version mentions AI (AI & Security Observatory). Bessent says the H200 policy is unchanged while NVIDIA says it stopped selling H200s to China, and the overseas data center tax has no rate or legal authority.

My call (Certain on what officials said (Reuters; CNBC); Guessing on whether any guardrail becomes enforceable)
No written guardrail text appears before January, and the overseas tax stays a threat (Guessing). Chip policy will keep moving by statement, not by rule.
Who wins
Chinese buyers of domestic chips, US cloud operators if the tax stays unwritten, and diplomats who want a talking point.
Who loses
Southeast Asian clouds caught by license rules, and planners who need a written rule.

5. Oracle says its OpenAI contract is fully funded, ahead of any contrary report.

Oracle said Friday it has secured all the funding needed for its OpenAI contract after a final capital raise this month, and expects positive free cash flow in the second half of fiscal 2028.

+Bull

A hyperscaler with the largest single AI contract says the money is in hand, Abilene is nearly complete, and the Shackelford campus is fully permitted with NVIDIA Vera Rubin racks arriving in weeks (Oracle).

−Bear

A company that pre-denies unnamed media reports invites the question of what those reports say, and vendor financing is mentioned as already in place. Oracle's debt, and its loan slices that traded below 90 cents on the dollar last week, remain the stress point.

My call (Certain on what Oracle said (Oracle statement); Guessing on any contrary report, which this brief did not find)
Oracle's funding holds through year end, but the next stress shows up in loan prices, not in its statements (Guessing).
Who wins
OpenAI, NVIDIA, and Oracle's construction partners.
Who loses
Short sellers and lenders that sold slices at a discount.

Anthropic's profit is an adjusted number: under standard accounting it lost $1.26 billion from operations

The prospectus shows adjusted operating income of $4.08 billion for the first nine months of 2026, which leaves out stock pay, noncash charitable gifts, and payments tied to a copyright settlement. Thinking Machines releases an open model that runs offline on a laptop and tops small open models at coding

Thinking Machines released Inkling 2 Mini on Nov 20 with open weights: 26.4 billion parameters in total, 3.4 billion active per token, about 20 gigabytes of memory, and a looped design that runs the middle layers three times per token. OpenAI says new protections cut successful model copying from 54 percent to 0.2 percent, with no outside test

OpenAI published research on Nov 20 on adversarial distillation, the practice of copying a model's capabilities, including its reasoning, without its safeguards. 1. Labs keep shipping while outside groups check their work
Frontier labs keep releasing new models while regulators, courts, and outside researchers check their work, rather than pausing on their own. Key evidence: Thinking Machines released an open model with monitorability tests of its own reasoning (Certain); OpenAI published research on stopping copying of its models, and reported its own 54.0 to 0.2 percent result with no outside test (Likely); Anthropic's prospectus describes recursive self-improvement as a goal and Sacks called it a policy concern (Likely); Trump and Xi agreed on guardrails with no text (Certain on the statements). 2. Demand for compute keeps compounding
Buyers keep needing more computing power, and cheaper models so far add to demand instead of cutting it. Key evidence: Server makers asked for up to 45 percent more on signed contracts and buyers are paying (FT, Likely); Anthropic's run-rate revenue rose about $10 billion in a month to about $81 billion (prospectus); Oracle says its OpenAI contract is funded and racks are arriving (Oracle).
