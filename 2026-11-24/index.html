02|The Big Three

NVIDIA posts the biggest one-day gain in US market history, then gives back about 6 percent as Trump says "I made the deal"

What it is: NVIDIA closed Monday at $165.05, up 31.48 percent, adding about $952 billion to a value near $3.98 trillion; MarketWatch called it the biggest single-day gain for any US stock (The Guardian). The Nasdaq rose 6.51 percent, its best day since April 2025, Micron rose 19.04 percent, Intel 16.34 percent, and AMD 6.85 percent, while Anthropic fell 1.43 percent to $87.64 (CNBC; Bullstory, from Yahoo Finance data). President Trump told CNBC "I made the deal" and "I convinced them," saying the board asked for assurances that Huang was not a national security risk; three people familiar with the board's thinking told CNBC a personal promise from Trump on Sunday sealed the decision, and the Financial Times reports that a group of Huang's allies lobbied Trump and Vice President JD Vance (Ars Technica, republishing the FT). By Tuesday morning NVIDIA traded about 6 percent lower (CNBC). Huang is due for a closed-door House interview on Tue Dec 1 and public testimony before the Senate Banking Committee on Thu Dec 3, and Senator Elizabeth Warren called the reinstatement "reckless" (CNBC).

Why it matters: The record gain shows how much of the AI trade sits on one company's leadership, and the president's role turns a board decision into a political one. Tuesday's fade suggests investors now price scrutiny instead: Bank of America wrote that "reinstatement should not be confused with the end of scrutiny" (CNBC).

Control check: If Monday's rebound meant investors trust AI demand again, it should hold a second day and lift the labs as well as the chips, and neither happened: NVIDIA traded about 6 percent lower Tuesday morning, with AMD down nearly 9 percent and Micron 8 percent (CNBC), and Anthropic fell 1.43 percent on Monday while chip stocks soared (Bloomberg), so the move priced one company's leadership, not lab profits (Likely).

Commerce blacklists Megaspeed and six affiliates, and Malaysia tells its data centers to cut them off

What it is: The Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) filed a rule Monday adding seven entities in Malaysia, Singapore, and Thailand to its Entity List, including Megaspeed International, its owner Swiftdata, its Malaysian unit Speedmatrix, and Apex Enterprise Solutions, Alibaba's Singapore subsidiary; exports of all items subject to the Export Administration Regulations (EAR) now need a license, reviewed with a presumption of denial (Federal Register public inspection; publication set for Wed Nov 25). Reuters reports, citing one unnamed person, that Apex paid about $4.2 billion to Megaspeed and others as a prepayment for computing capacity in Southeast Asia and took some of their assets as a pledge; Alibaba called the listing "erroneous" and said it will "seek all legal avenues." Malaysia's trade ministry said its investment agency will advise every data center operator not to host, lease space to, or serve the seven firms (Ministry of Investment, Trade and Industry, or MITI). Singapore Prime Minister Lawrence Wong said Tuesday that Singapore is working with the US on a mechanism "where America can do its own enforcement," and that of the information Singapore requested, "not all of it was forthcoming" (The Straits Times).

Why it matters: This is the first US action aimed at the graphics processing unit (GPU) clouds of Southeast Asia rather than at chips or whole countries, and Malaysia's notice turns a US list into a local hosting ban. Prepaid capacity deals, a common way to finance GPU clouds, now carry the risk that a customer or a landlord lands on a list.

Control check: If the listing truly cuts Megaspeed off from NVIDIA chips, the rule should apply the foreign direct product rule, because NVIDIA's chips are made in Taiwan; the public inspection text lists only "all items subject to the EAR" with no such clause, and Chris McGuire argues on X that the rule "has ZERO effect," while the Bureau of Industry and Security's spokesman told Semafor that chips produced outside the United States are covered, so the reach stays disputed until the published version appears Wednesday (Likely).

Applied Digital signs a $5.4 billion, 18-year hyperscaler lease at higher prices, while funding its build with a one-year loan

What it is: Applied Digital said Monday that an unnamed tier-one, investment-grade hyperscaler signed an 18-year lease for 150 megawatts of critical information technology (IT) capacity: about $5.4 billion of base-term revenue, or about $12.7 billion if all renewals run the full 33 years, with "higher pricing" than its earlier leases. Contracted base-term revenue now exceeds $41 billion across more than 1.6 gigawatts, about 75 percent of it from investment-grade tenants, and delivery begins in the first half of 2028 (Applied Digital). The company also closed a $300 million delayed-draw bridge loan with a 364-day maturity for its Louisiana campus (MarketWatch) and plans project financing for two other campuses in the first calendar quarter of 2027. The same day AMD said Microsoft has powered on its first AMD Helios rack-scale system at an Azure lab and is running early workloads for validation (AMD), and CoreWeave said chief executive Mike Intrator sees customers "asking for more and more infrastructure" (CoreWeave on X).

Why it matters: Hyperscalers are still signing leases that run nearly two decades, at rising prices, a week after the Nasdaq fell 10 percent (CNBC), which is demand compounding in contract form. The weak link is timing: the tenant starts paying in 2028, while the builder borrows on a one-year clock now.

Control check: If hyperscaler demand is still rising, other landlords should be signing too, and one more appears: an environmental filing shows Amazon Web Services (AWS) and Greece's Public Power Corporation planning a 300 megawatt campus in Kozani that could grow to 1 gigawatt, with a final agreement expected by the first quarter of 2027 (DCD); but Applied Digital did not name its tenant and neither deal names chips, so the demand is contracted but not yet visible in hardware orders (Likely).

Questions worth asking

Three testable questions from today's news, each with the evidence that would answer it.

1. If Commerce can list a chip cloud but may not stop chips made abroad from reaching it, what actually changes for Megaspeed first: its chip supply, its hosting, or its financing?

What would answer it: The Federal Register version published Wednesday (whether a foreign direct product clause appears), Malaysia's notice to data center operators and any evictions that follow, and whether the roughly $4.2 billion Alibaba's Apex unit prepaid for capacity is frozen or fought over in court.

2. When a landlord signs an 18-year lease at higher prices but funds the build with a 364-day loan, who carries the gap if the tenant's plans change before 2028?

What would answer it: Applied Digital's project finance terms in the first quarter of 2027, the lease's termination and credit support terms in its next quarterly filing, and what lenders charge to replace the $300 million bridge.

3. If Goldman Sachs is right that the memory shortfall shrinks from 5.1 percent in 2027 to 1.2 percent in 2028, which buyers are locking in 2027 prices that will look expensive, and who absorbs the loss?

What would answer it: Contract memory prices for the first half of 2027, ChangXin Memory Technologies output figures, Samsung and SK hynix comments on long-term supply agreements, and whether server makers keep their repricing clauses once prices turn.

03|Use Cases
What superintelligence is actually doing in the world, and how well it works.

PostNord's AI phone line now resolves 60 percent of Swedish customer calls, up from 45 percent

Who and what: PostNord, the Nordic postal and logistics operator, replaced the AI-based phone system that answered every inbound customer call in Sweden with a multi-agent system built on ElevenLabs' ElevenAgents: an orchestrator routes each caller to one of four specialized subagents, including a tracking agent kept on a lighter, cheaper setup, and live traffic is split between configurations for testing. PostNord had earlier used in-house intent models and language models from several providers; the model, chips, and cloud now in use: not disclosed. Published Mon Nov 23.

Measured result: Calls resolved without escalation rose from 45 percent to 60 percent, which the companies describe as a 33 percent increase, and the old system was fully decommissioned in about 90 days. Tag: Likely (vendor case study with customer quotes; no outside audit, and call volume not published).

Strength and weakness: Strength: natural Swedish across regional dialects, around-the-clock answers, and a four-phase rollout with a go or no-go review before each step. Weakness: 40 percent of calls still escalate or fail, telephony problems needed joint fixes with Telia, and the figures come from the vendor.

Cost and time to deploy: Not disclosed; PostNord's automation lead cites "real cost savings" without a figure. About 90 days from start to full production in Sweden, with Denmark, Norway, and Finland next.

Maturity: Production

A London hospital's autonomous AI discharged a quarter to a third of urgent skin cancer referrals without a clinician

Who and what: Chelsea and Westminster Hospital, part of England's National Health Service (NHS), ran an autonomous AI teledermatology pathway, a CE-marked AI medical device, for urgent suspected skin cancer referrals across two hospitals for 16 months, supported by its CW Innovation programme. The study poster is hosted by Skin Analytics, whose file names its DERM product. Chips and cloud: not disclosed. The trust presented the study on Nov 12 at the European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology Congress, and HTN Health Tech News reported it on Tue Nov 24, inside this window.

Measured result: The pathway handled 8,391 patients, 94 percent of urgent referrals, and 86 percent consented to autonomous decisions. The AI discharged 31 percent and 25 percent of patients at the two hospitals without clinician review, routine follow-up fell from 27 to 12 percent against standard teledermatology, and biopsies ran at 27 percent against 43 percent in face-to-face care. The trust estimates 2,851 clinician hours saved, about a 62 percent capacity gain, or more than 8,500 extra 20-minute appointments; the poster reports 6 false-negative cases discharged among 8,391 patients. Tag: Likely (trust release and study poster; a prospective real-world study, not a randomized trial).

Strength and weakness: Strength: the researchers call it the first large data set from a prospective real-world deployment of autonomous AI in a cancer pathway, and the saved hours go to patients with cancer. Weakness: the hours saved are an estimate against face-to-face care, 14 percent of patients did not consent to autonomous decisions, and darker skin types were still routed to a clinician.

Cost and time to deploy: Not disclosed.

Maturity: Production

Dwelly's AI agents now handle 55 percent of the steps in letting a UK home, and negotiator hours per letting halved

Who and what: Dwelly, which buys traditional UK letting agencies and runs them on its own software (20 agencies, more than 17,000 properties), uses Anthropic's Claude and OpenAI's models through their business application programming interfaces on its own UK-hosted infrastructure. Agents decide when applications go to landlords, tell applicants the outcome, and chase holding deposits; a person confirms consequential decisions. Chips: not disclosed. Co-founder Dan Lifshits told TNW on Mon Nov 23, in a story published a few hours before this window opened.

Measured result: About 55 percent of steps run with no human touch (a September estimate), manual work per letting fell from 28 touches to about 13, negotiator time per letting from 6.5 to 3.2 hours, applicants answered within an hour from 35 to about 90 percent, offer to paid deposit from five days to 2.4, and lettings that fall through from 14 to 9 percent. Tag: Likely (company figures given to a reporter; no audit).

Strength and weakness: Strength: a stated rule that "autonomy is earned," with landlord relationships kept by people. Weakness: the company says a real before-and-after takes six to twelve months from April and that it will not know for a year whether placed tenants pay on time; effects on headcount are not stated.

Cost and time to deploy: Not disclosed; the agent rollout began in April 2026, and the company has raised $263 million in total.

Maturity: Production

Malaysia's hosting notice does more to cut Megaspeed off than the US rule itself, and Commerce issues a correction or clarification on foreign-made chips within weeks (Guessing).
Who wins
Clouds in the region that can prove clean customers, US enforcers, and Malaysia's standing with Washington.
Who loses
Megaspeed, Apex and Alibaba, and data center landlords with Megaspeed-linked tenants.

5. Labs hand agents more authority: code approvals and live system shields.

Anthropic launched Claude Ship, including a beta agent that approves routine code changes under a team's written policy, and OpenAI and Cisco open-sourced Shirudo, a Linux runtime that lets agents write temporary shields against exploits on running systems.

+Bull

Review and patching are now the bottleneck, so agents that approve, fix, and shield remove real delays; in a controlled test a Codex-generated shield blocked a Java4Shell exploit against a running Elasticsearch service without a restart (OpenAI). Shirudo is open source under a BSD license, so outsiders can inspect it.

−Bear

Both tools move authority from people to agents: Claude Approvals is in beta and Anthropic suggests running it in observe-only mode first (TechCrunch), and Shirudo's result comes from a controlled test. Agents with kernel-level controls raise the stakes of containment while METR and Redwood Research have yet to publish their review of the Hugging Face incident.

My call (Certain on the launches (TechCrunch; OpenAI); Guessing on adoption and error rates)
Approval agents spread first at teams that already write most code with agents, and the first public mistake shapes rules faster than any bill (Guessing).
Who wins
Anthropic, OpenAI, Cisco, and security teams with long patch queues.
Who loses
Manual review steps and security vendors that sell patch delays as a service.

The Megaspeed rule text has no clause for chips made abroad, while Commerce says those chips are covered

An Entity List entry reaches chips made abroad only when its license requirement carries a foreign direct product footnote, as Chris McGuire explains in a post on X, and the public inspection text for all seven new entries reads only "For all items subject to the EAR" with a presumption of denial. NVIDIA's chips are made in Taiwan, which is why McGuire argues that the rule has "ZERO effect" on chip sales to Megaspeed. The Bureau of Industry and Security's spokesman told Semafor the opposite: that exporting advanced chips to Megaspeed is now unlawful "even if the chips are produced outside the United States." The published version Wednesday, or a correction, settles which is true. Malaysia's hosting notice applies either way.

Confidence: Likely (Federal Register public inspection text; Semafor; McGuire on X; legal reach not confirmed by Commerce in writing)

Goldman Sachs sees the memory shortage nearly gone in 2028, with commodity DRAM prices falling about 20 percent

Goldman's Nov 22 note cut its Samsung target 30 percent to 350,000 won and its SK hynix target about 43 percent to 2 million won while keeping Buy ratings. The bank sees the DRAM supply shortfall at 5.1 percent in 2027 narrowing to 1.2 percent in 2028 as China's ChangXin Memory Technologies ramps and personal computer and smartphone demand slows, with commodity DRAM prices down 20 and 19 percent for the two companies in 2028 and NAND flash about 20 percent lower. High-bandwidth memory keeps growing, from $60 billion this year to $200 billion in 2027 and $237 billion in 2028. Morgan Stanley made similar cuts on Nov 9. That view cuts against last week's JPMorgan note, which saw only 60 to 70 percent of 2027 demand met.

Baidu quietly posts free-to-use weights for a 280 billion parameter model that scores near the frontier on web browsing

Three repositories for ERNIE 5.5 Flash Preview appeared on Baidu's Hugging Face page on Nov 23 with no announcement, two days before Baidu's own target, and were reported Tuesday. The license is plain Apache 2.0, more permissive than the base model's, which excludes the European Union, the United Kingdom, and South Korea and caps free commercial use. The model has 280 billion parameters with 14 billion active per token and a context window of about 1 million tokens. Baidu's own runs show 87.4 on BrowseComp and 71.2 on DeepSWE v1.1, but 46.5 on Terminal-Bench 4.0 against 66.4 for Claude Opus 5.5; no outside group has rerun them. Baidu's hosted price was reported at $0.28 per million input tokens and $1.13 per million output tokens, and the smallest download is 166 gigabytes, so this runs on a multi-GPU server, not a laptop.
