02|The Big Three

Anthropic jumps 17 percent on its first day while AI stocks keep falling

What it is: Anthropic's Class A shares opened at $111.50 on Thursday, touched $115.10, and closed at $97.17, up 17.1 percent from the $83 offer price, with more than 846 million shares traded and a fully diluted value near $1.8 trillion at the close (CNBC; Yahoo Finance). Anthropic's closing notice, dated Friday, says underwriters bought all 147 million extra shares, lifting the offering to $93.5 billion, of which Anthropic received about $91.6 billion; with the $26.0 billion private placement to AMD, Broadcom, Google, Micron, Microsoft, NVIDIA, and TSMC, gross proceeds were about $119.5 billion. The rest of the market went the other way: the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 1,101 points, the S&P 500 lost 2 percent for a sixth straight loss and sits 8.2 percent below its October high, and CoreWeave swung from a 10.5 percent morning gain to a loss of more than 5 percent (AP; CBS News). In his listing day remarks, Dario Amodei pledged to "pace the training and deployment of AI systems" until Anthropic is confident they are safe and under human control.

Why it matters: Demand for owning a frontier lab is real and measurable, but the market did not extend it to the chip, memory, and cloud companies that supply that lab. Financing for labs is easing while financing for the companies that build their capacity is not, and a public pledge to slow down now has shareholders watching whether it holds.

Control check: If public investors truly want AI exposure, the rest of the chain should have rallied with Anthropic, and it did not: NVIDIA fell about 3.3 percent, AMD 7.7 percent, CoreWeave more than 5 percent, and the S&P 500 lost its sixth day in a row (CNBC; CBS News), so the appetite was for the scarce lab, not the suppliers (Likely).

Washington approved 879,868 NVIDIA and AMD chips for China, and AMD drops 7.7 percent on a short report

What it is: The House Foreign Affairs Committee, led by Chairman Brian Mast, released Commerce Department license data as of May 11, 2026: companies requested 3,444,160 chips for Chinese buyers and the Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) approved 879,868 NVIDIA H200 and AMD MI325X chips across 21 licenses, including 75,000 for Alibaba, 150,000 for Tencent, 43,000 for Baidu, and 4,000 for BYD, four firms on the Department of War's list of Chinese military companies. Every approved row is marked "Approved, not shipped," and NVIDIA told The Hill it has since stopped shipping H200s to China. The same day, Hunterbrook Media disclosed a short position in AMD and alleged that newer AMD firmware kept flowing to blacklisted server maker IEIT Systems and that AMD bills more than 20 percent of revenue to China and Hong Kong; AMD said the claims misrepresent its business, and its shares closed down 7.72 percent at $489.19 (CNBC, which did not verify the report). A Commerce rule that took effect Thursday also requires licenses for advanced chips going to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Brazil (Federal Register).

Why it matters: China is now a political liability as well as a revenue line for both chip makers, and the rules are tightening around the countries that used to serve as workarounds. AMD, which investors had treated as the alternative to NVIDIA, now faces the same scrutiny as NVIDIA, just as NVIDIA's own guidance already assumes no China data center sales.

Control check: If 879,868 chips were really sold to China, the revenue should show in company results and the table should say shipped; NVIDIA guides to zero China data center compute and says it stopped shipping H200s, and every approved row in the House table reads "Approved, not shipped," so the Reuters word "shipments" overstates what the record shows (Likely).

The Senate passes a bill that makes big data centers pay for the grid they use

What it is: On Thursday the Senate passed H.R. 2347, the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, by 62 to 33, with Republicans Josh Hawley and Mike Rounds voting no (U.S. Senate press office). The bill speeds permitting reviews and requires data centers and other high-density computing sites of 20 megawatts or more to pay the full added cost of generation, transmission, and distribution built to serve them, with financial assurances before construction, and it directs the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) to update transmission pricing (Edit USA; Roll Call). The Senate amended the bill, so the House must act again. The same evening Reuters reported that a planned 1.44 gigawatt data center in Queensland, Australia, with Anthropic as anchor tenant, drew 595 objections out of nearly 600 public submissions, with two in favor (Straits Times).

Why it matters: Power is shifting from a technical limit to a cost that builders pay up front, which favors buyers with deep balance sheets and slows everyone else. Local consent is becoming a second constraint, and it now reaches the largest lab right after its record raise.

Control check: If power and local consent truly limit AI builds, other projects should be slipping or losing lenders, and they are: HSBC sold a $300 million slice of the $18 billion Project Jupiter loan below 90 cents on the dollar after Oracle's September delay notice, and Microsoft dropped a Nscale site over power (Bisnow; Nscale filing), so the Senate vote and the Australian objections fit a pattern (Likely).

Questions worth asking

Three testable questions from today's news, each with the evidence that would answer it.

1. Do the China approvals ever become shipped revenue, and where else would shipments show up first: the licensing record, company filings, or Southeast Asian customs data?

What would answer it: BIS shipment records for the 21 licenses, the China lines in NVIDIA's and AMD's next filings, whether NVIDIA's $130 billion fourth-quarter guide keeps China data center compute at zero, and any Commerce reply to the House release.

2. When a data center must pay the full grid cost, does the price of a rented graphics processing unit (GPU) hour rise, or do household power bills simply stop rising: who really carries the bill?

What would answer it: Utility tariff filings after any House vote, neocloud and cloud contract prices signed after the rule, and how PJM (the largest US grid operator) and the Electric Reliability Council of Texas assign costs to loads of 20 megawatts or more.

3. What metric would prove Anthropic paced its training rather than only said it would: dated delays, skipped releases, or capacity left unused?

What would answer it: Release dates and system cards that cite a safety hold, updates to its Responsible Scaling Policy, capacity committed versus used in its first quarterly report, and checks by METR or government AI institutes.

03|Use Cases
What superintelligence is actually doing in the world, and how well it works.

Bogotá's city line answers in 2 seconds, down from 2 minutes 5 seconds

Who and what: Línea 195, the Bogotá District's 24 hour line for city services, complaints, and guidance, now uses an AI voice agent named Alex for inbound calls. Outsourcing S.A.S. BIC and its AI spinoff DipDig built it on SoundHound's OASYS agent platform. The agent serves nearly 8 million residents across 76 district entities, answers water, tax, and mobility questions, and ties every answer to one of more than 16,000 official documents, with transcripts, sources, and routes saved for review. Model, chips, and cloud: not disclosed.

Measured result: Average wait fell from 2 minutes 5 seconds to 2 seconds, satisfaction rose from 2.8 to 4.8 out of 5 since launch, and the agent resolves 91 percent of inquiries on its trained topics without a transfer to a person. Tag: Likely (SoundHound release of Nov 19; no method or audit published).

Strength and weakness: Strength: grounding in a single official source, saved transcripts, handoff of complex or sensitive cases to people, and up to 1,000 calls at once. Weakness: the first phase covers information and guidance only, with filings, bookings, and payments to come later, the 91 percent applies only to trained topics, and the satisfaction scores come from the vendor.

Cost and time to deploy: Not disclosed; launch date not stated.

Maturity: Production.

OpenAI's coding agents root-cause 600 ChatGPT slowdowns in three months

Who and what: OpenAI's performance engineers built the Performance Factory, a cloud system where Codex agents run recurring jobs. When a performance alert fires, the Regression Workflow opens a task, an agent investigates and records findings, proposes a fix for an engineer to review, and a second agent checks after deployment that speed returned to baseline. The team encoded its investigation habits into a shared skill and more than 30 performance tools. Chips and cloud: not disclosed.

Measured result: In just over three months the workflow tracked more than 1,700 investigations and root-caused over 600, about 17 investigations a day. Tag: Certain that OpenAI published these counts (OpenAI, Nov 19); no accuracy rate or engineer time saved was published.

Strength and weakness: Strength: the whole loop from alert to verification runs without a person starting it. Weakness: engineers still review and merge each fix, which OpenAI says is now the bottleneck, and roughly one in three investigations ended in a root cause by our arithmetic.

Cost and time to deploy: Not disclosed.

Maturity: Production (internal).

Tencent releases Hy4, a 770 billion parameter open model with a million-token memory

Tencent released the weights for Hy4 on Nov 20 under the Apache 2.0 license, including FP8 and base versions: a 770 billion parameter mixture of experts model that uses 49 billion parameters per token, with a context window of 1 million tokens, aimed at long agent tasks, coding, and computer use. Together AI began hosting it Thursday evening Pacific Time, so US customers can use it without touching Chinese servers.
