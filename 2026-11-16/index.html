02|The Big Three

NVIDIA's succession fight reaches Arm's chief executive

What it is: The Financial Times (FT) reported Friday that NVIDIA's board approached Arm chief executive Rene Haas, who spent seven years at NVIDIA before joining Arm in 2013, as one of several candidates to succeed Jensen Huang. The board said it is still selecting a search firm, has not formally engaged any candidate, and called speculation premature; Arm declined to comment. On Sunday the FT reported that Huang has discussed a return with confidants while allies assemble a 'war room' of Silicon Valley executives to lobby the board and seek White House support. A Stand with Jensen petition passed about 180,000 signatures, and Altimeter's Brad Gerstner and Atreides' Gavin Baker asked the board to keep all options open, including Huang's return. Arm shares closed down 4.63 percent at $190.53 on Friday.

Why it matters: NVIDIA reports Tuesday under interim chief executive Colette Kress, and the leadership question now touches its closest partners: NVIDIA's Grace and Vera processors are built on Arm's architecture. A Huang return would have to clear the board's own independent probe and congressional interviews starting December 1, so the likeliest near-term outcome is a long search, not a quick pick (Guessing).

Washington closes the remote chip-rental loophole

What it is: The Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) issued an interim final rule Monday, written at the direction of the Treasury Department and the National Security Council, that requires a license to ship advanced AI chips to data centers anywhere that would supply a significant volume of computing power to companies headquartered in China, Macau, or other restricted countries. The control covers data centers or networks above 12 million total processing performance (TPP), adds license requirements for advanced chips to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Brazil except for US-headquartered buyers that report, exempts commercial recommendation systems, gives existing operators 90 days to get licenses, and formally rescinds the never-enforced AI Diffusion Rule. Separately, The Wall Street Journal reported that the administration plans to name more than two dozen firms, including NVIDIA, Amazon, Microsoft, OpenAI, and Anthropic, as AI 'national champions' with faster export licenses and access to federal financing.

Why it matters: Policy now decides who can rent compute, not just who can buy chips. Neoclouds and colocation operators in Southeast Asia must vet who owns their customers, US hyperscalers keep an easier path in the five named countries, and the rule lands a day before NVIDIA reports.

Anthropic calls compute its deepest dilemma days before listing

What it is: At a JPMorgan event Friday, Anthropic chief executive Dario Amodei told JPMorgan chief executive Jamie Dimon that 'compute is the deepest business dilemma that the company faces,' because Anthropic must buy capacity years ahead without knowing how fast revenue will follow. He said the company secures relatively conservative amounts in advance and relies on its cash and the spot market if demand runs ahead. Its filings show commitments of nearly $901 billion, mostly chips and computing infrastructure, with roughly $71 billion due within the year ending September 30, 2027 (Likely). Anthropic is raising up to $64.4 billion in the offering plus a $26 billion private placement and plans to start trading Thursday.

Why it matters: The largest compute buyer outside the hyperscalers is telling public investors that sizing its chip orders is its hardest call, and each order it books or trims moves Google, Broadcom, NVIDIA, and the neoclouds. JPMorgan strategists expect AI spending to push US investment-grade bond issuance to a record $2.1 trillion in 2027.

03|Use Cases
What superintelligence is actually doing in the world, and how well it works.

CVS Health's agents clear a months-long backlog in a weekend

Who and what: CVS Health uses OpenAI's computer-use agents, which click, type, and move between applications, to send documents, process referrals, and support specialist consults across clinical systems that its application programming interfaces (APIs) do not fully cover, per an OpenAI post on November 16. Data stays in CVS's own enterprise-controlled infrastructure, and CVS is open-sourcing its Agent Kit built on OpenAI's Agents software development kit. Cigna Healthcare uses the same capability to pull member context from more than 10 systems for case managers. Model: OpenAI's GPT-6 family; chips and cloud: not disclosed.

Measured result: In one production workflow an agent cleared a months-long document-sending backlog over a weekend; another handles the system steps for tens of thousands of consults each day. No hours saved or error rates were published for CVS. In OpenAI's own evaluation, 99 percent of 325 reviewed clinical inbox tasks were rated safe and physicians rated the work very or extremely helpful in 93 percent of 646 assessments. Tag: Likely (company-reported).

Strength and weakness: Strength: agents reach legacy systems with no API, under validation checks, screenshots, and action records. Weakness: humans still review the work, and the CVS gains are described, not measured in hours or dollars.

Cost and time to deploy: Not disclosed for CVS. On a Stanford healthcare administration benchmark, GPT-6 Luna scored 91.8 percent at 8.5 cents per task, 98 percent cheaper than Astra's 94.3 percent; Cigna went from concept to deployment in under eight weeks.

Maturity: Production.

A physicist and GPT-6 Astra propose a lattice definition of the Standard Model

Who and what: Nathaniel Craig, who leads the particle theory group at the University of California, Santa Barbara and is a visiting scientist at OpenAI, worked with GPT-6 Astra on a candidate nonperturbative lattice definition of the Standard Model of particle physics, described in the first post on OpenAI's science blog on November 15 and an October preprint. Chips and cloud: not disclosed.

Measured result: Craig writes that Astra suggested how to carry force fields onto finer grids, showed the comparisons converge, and took the lead in developing the detailed proof of a phase rule that had been open for more than 25 years. Lattice theorists Maarten Golterman, Andreas Kronfeld, and Yigal Shamir reviewed the proof. Tag: Certain that the claim was made; Guessing on whether the result holds after peer review.

Strength and weakness: Strength: an AI system leading a proof on a decades-old physics problem, checked by named outside experts. Weakness: the result holds at finite lattice spacing under a bound on field strengths, reflection positivity is still unchecked, and Craig calls it an answer in principle, not yet in practice.

Cost and time to deploy: Not disclosed; several rounds of work with Astra between the October preprint and the November post.

Maturity: Pilot.

4. A judge lets the DRAM price-fixing case against the big three go forward.

Judge Noël Wise of the US District Court for the Northern District of California denied Samsung, SK hynix, and Micron's motion to dismiss a dynamic random access memory (DRAM) price-fixing class action in an order signed November 14. Answers are due December 7 and the first case management conference is January 12, 2027. Tom's Hardware reports the judge found collusion claims plausible after a roughly 500 percent price surge. Separately, memory maker TeamGroup's chief executive warned that the 2027 DRAM shortage could become so severe that even money will not guarantee supply.

+Bull

A pleading-stage ruling is not a finding of liability, and the memory makers can point to real AI demand for high-bandwidth memory (HBM) as the reason regular DRAM is short.

−Bear

Discovery into how capacity moved to HBM could expose pricing talks just as server, PC, and phone makers face shortages through 2027.

My call (Certain on the ruling and dates (court order); Likely on the price surge and TeamGroup's warning (Tom's Hardware; Wccftech))
No settlement before the January conference (Guessing). Memory stays the tightest input for AI servers through 2027.
Who wins
Buyers holding long-term memory contracts; plaintiffs' lawyers.
Who loses
Samsung, SK hynix, and Micron on legal cost and scrutiny; device makers buying DRAM on the spot market.

The new chip rule spares American companies in the five risky countries

Headlines called Monday's rule a crackdown on Southeast Asia, but the text says the new license requirement for advanced chips going to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Brazil does not apply to chips destined for companies headquartered in the United States, or whose ultimate parent is, provided they meet reporting requirements. Local operators and brokers carry the new burden, while US hyperscaler regions in those countries keep an easier path.

Confidence: Certain on the text (Bureau of Industry and Security rule); Guessing on the share shift

The size threshold catches mid-size clusters, not just giant ones

The remote-access control applies to data centers or networks above 12 million total processing performance (TPP), which TechCrunch estimates at roughly the peak computing power of about 750 NVIDIA H100 or H200 chips. That is a modest cluster by 2026 standards, so many regional neoclouds fall inside it, and existing operators serving covered customers have 90 days to get licenses. The same rule formally rescinds the never-enforced AI Diffusion Rule.

Confidence: Certain on the threshold and timing (Bureau of Industry and Security); Likely on the chip-count estimate (TechCrunch)

Anthropic's 'conservative' buying still adds up to $901 billion

Amodei told Dimon that Anthropic secures relatively conservative amounts of compute in advance and relies on the spot market if demand outruns plans. Its filings show commitments of nearly $901 billion, heavily weighted to chips and computing infrastructure, with roughly $71 billion due within the year ending September 30, 2027. Conservative here means relative to its own growth forecasts, not relative to any other buyer in history.

07|Unknown unknowns
Surprises from outside the big players. Each tagged Certain, Likely, or Guessing.

A black market for stolen frontier model access

Security firm Wiz counts at least nine AI developer platforms that disclosed breaches in October, including Fireworks AI, Railway, OpenCode, and Northflank, with more since, among them NVIDIA's Brev and Trigger.dev. Attackers hunted stored AI keys. In two cases Wiz traced, one stolen key made about 800,000 Amazon Bedrock calls in 30 hours and another about 1.7 million in 13 hours, mostly Claude Opus models, likely more than $100,000 at list price, and the keys surfaced with resale sites selling discounted frontier access. On Monday Gergely Orosz wrote that the same wave also hit Bitrise, which the company has not confirmed. Stolen access is hidden demand for frontier tokens and a new cost for every inference platform.

Confidence: Certain on Wiz's findings and the company disclosures (Wiz); Guessing on Orosz's Bitrise claim and attribution
