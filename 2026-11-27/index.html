02|The Big Three

Bloomberg finds an NVIDIA Blackwell rack in a Shanghai data center tied to Alice Huang, pledged to a local bank as collateral

What it is: Bloomberg reported Friday that two Chinese firms installed NVIDIA Blackwell hardware at the Yangtze River Delta Project in Shanghai, a site Megaspeed showed investors in 2024 as its largest data center. Documents filed with Chinese regulators in November list a GB300 NVL72 rack, 72 Blackwell Ultra chips and 36 Grace processors, by serial number; a public warranty database ties it to Taiwan's Gigabyte. Shanghai Shuoyao pledged the rack for a 96 million yuan ($14 million) loan from Shanghai Innovation Bank, and a second firm, Shanghai Kezhan, described a GB200 server there when it borrowed 90 million yuan in late 2025; its filings also list 32 servers with H200 chips (Bloomberg; Quartz). Bloomberg says it has no evidence Megaspeed itself diverted the hardware; Megaspeed denies wrongdoing, Gigabyte declined to say who bought the rack, and NVIDIA did not respond. Separately, Reuters reported that Z.AI's next flagship model has slipped and Moonshot expects its release cycle to stretch from six months to a year or more if it loses overseas computing (Reuters via Yahoo Finance).

Why it matters: A rack that weighs more than 1,500 kilograms, and that Huang once called too big to smuggle, now sits on a Chinese bank's collateral list, so the leak is documented in paperwork rather than rumor. That hands Congress a concrete exhibit for Huang's closed-door House interview on Dec 1 and his Senate testimony on Dec 3, and explains why NVIDIA's partners stopped shipping to four Southeast Asian countries this week.

Control check: If the export rules now bite, Chinese labs should slow down, and they do: Reuters reports Z.AI's GLM-5.5 missed its November target and Moonshot expects its gap between major releases to double without overseas computing; but no public record shows who first bought the Gigabyte rack or whether US enforcement follows, so the size of the leak is unknown (Likely).

Tokyo Electric plans about $64 billion for grids and reactors to win US cloud data centers

What it is: Nikkei reported Thursday that Tokyo Electric Power Company Holdings (TEPCO) will invest ¥10.2 trillion, about $64 billion, through fiscal 2035: ¥4.9 trillion to expand transmission and distribution and ¥2.5 trillion for nuclear power, with cumulative spending above ¥15 trillion by fiscal 2040, to attract data centers from US cloud providers such as Amazon Web Services (AWS) (Nikkei via SBS and JKN). TEPCO expects data center power sales to grow nearly eightfold by fiscal 2035, wants to charge data centers more than standard rates, and is negotiating capital tie-ups with five groups including SoftBank, KKR, and Blackstone, because it lacks the money to fund the build itself (JKN; Crypto Briefing). The extra revenue would help cover about ¥500 billion a year of Fukushima Daiichi cleanup and compensation. The same day TEPCO said a required safety facility for reactor 7 at Kashiwazaki-Kariwa will be more than a year late (Dan Nystedt on X).

Why it matters: Power is now the product being sold: a utility plans to raise outside money to build wires and reactors before it has signed a tenant. The plan rests on reactor restarts that have slipped before, so the bottleneck shifts from chips to construction schedules and local consent.

Control check: If power is the binding limit, other regulators and landlords should be moving too, and they are: Texas resumed data center permits on Nov 16 and cleared SpaceX's 750 megawatt gas plant (Texas Tribune; Politico), and KT Cloud leased all 60 megawatts of Keppel's Ansan site (Keppel); but TEPCO has named no signed cloud tenant and has not published the plan itself, so the demand side is unconfirmed (Likely).

Regulators license SpaceX for up to 1 million data center satellites, with the first one due by 2033

What it is: The Federal Communications Commission (FCC) Space Bureau granted in part and deferred in part SpaceX's application for Starmind, a system of up to 1,000,000 satellites operating as data centers at 500 to 1,002 kilometers. The order, released Nov 25 and widely shared on X Thursday, covers only backup and emergency radio links for tracking and control, because the satellites would talk by laser, and sets deadlines: one unit by Nov 25, 2033, 10 percent by 2035, half by 2038, and the full system by 2040 (FCC order; Space Law Journal). Satellites above 600 kilometers that would not burn up on reentry need a separate waiver first. Amazon, Viasat, and the American Astronomical Society opposed the plan (CNBC). Ars Technica noted the decision "does not establish that orbital data centers are technically or economically viable."

Why it matters: The company that now owns xAI has a legal path to move some computing off the power grid, the boldest answer yet to the power bottleneck. The deadlines say that answer is years away: the first unit is due in 2033, while SpaceX's 750 megawatts of gas turbines at its $24 billion Starbase campus in Texas are already permitted.

Control check: If orbital computing were close, we should see chip orders, launch dates, or paying customers, and we do not: a widely shared post says the first satellites launch in 2027, but the FCC order requires only one unit by 2033 and names no chip supplier, cost, or customer (Guessing).

Questions worth asking

Three testable questions from today's news, each with the evidence that would answer it.

1. If a Shanghai firm could pledge a restricted GB300 rack to a local bank, are Chinese loan and leasing filings now the best public map of where restricted chips sit?

What would answer it: More Chinese lender or leasing filings that list Blackwell serial numbers, any Commerce enforcement that cites them, and Gigabyte's or NVIDIA's account of who first bought the rack.

2. TEPCO plans to charge data centers more than standard rates; what premium will a cloud provider pay for firm grid and nuclear power in Tokyo versus building its own gas turbines in Texas?

What would answer it: The price in TEPCO's first signed data center contract, the Electric Reliability Council of Texas (ERCOT) report on who pays for large loads on Dec 10, and published per-megawatt costs for on-site gas plants such as SpaceX's at Starbase.

3. If Moonshot now distills from Google and Meta models after OpenAI and Anthropic blocked it, are the labs' own safeguards a bigger brake on Chinese AI than export rules?

What would answer it: Statements from Google and Meta on distillation blocks, the release dates of GLM-5.5 and Kimi K4, and outside benchmark gaps between the next Chinese models and the models they learned from.

03|Use Cases
What superintelligence is actually doing in the world, and how well it works.

A Seoul gas utility cut a 3 to 4 week territory review to about 10 minutes with an AI-built map simulator

Who and what: Yesco, a city gas supplier, named the winners of its internal AI contest on Thu Nov 26. The top entry is a contract management system with a geographic information system (GIS) simulator that redraws which customer center serves which district on a map, replacing spreadsheet work. Staff built it themselves. Model, chips, and cloud: not disclosed.

Measured result: Yesco says the review that took 3 to 4 weeks in Excel now takes about 10 minutes, the in-house build avoided about 40 million won of outside GIS work, and 560 staff at 23 customer centers use it. A related valve inspection map went into night inspections last month. Tag: Likely (company figures reported by Pinpoint News; no outside audit).

Strength and weakness: Strength: a non-tech utility, built by front-line staff, with a clear before and after and real users. Weakness: the article does not say how much of the tool is AI versus ordinary mapping software, and contest winners are the best cases.

Cost and time to deploy: About 40 million won avoided; build cost and time not disclosed (contest ran from Sept 1).

Maturity: Production

A Korean city cut training certificate checks from 30 minutes to 5 with a tool a clerk built

Who and what: South Chungcheong Province held its public AI transformation challenge on Fri Nov 27. The top prize went to Nam Geun-woo of Asan City's information and communications office for a system that automatically verifies employees' training certificates and compiles the results. Other winners included a construction cost calculator for small resident projects in Boryeong. Model, chips, and cloud: not disclosed.

Measured result: Processing time for training completion records fell from 30 minutes to 5 minutes, per the province. Tag: Likely (government figure reported by Storyspot; no sample size or audit).

Strength and weakness: Strength: civil servants without technical training built and used the tools, and the province will share winners across cities. Weakness: one timing figure, no volume, and no error rate.

Cost and time to deploy: Not disclosed.

Maturity: Pilot

A 150-year-old British salt supplier puts an AI agent on 30,000 orders a year

Who and what: Peacock Salt, a family-owned supplier to food and other industries, went live with an order processing agent built by Glasgow's Agency AI, reported Fri Nov 27. Who loses
Gigabyte and other server assemblers facing questions; Chinese labs renting offshore; NVIDIA's China defense.

Fitch says data center landlords are shielded at first, and neoclouds with Chinese tenants take the hit

Fitch Ratings wrote Thursday that tighter US controls will make Asia-Pacific data center investment slower and more selective, and that neocloud providers relying on Chinese customers and US chips face the greatest credit risk because they need expensive hardware fully used.
