02|The Big Three

NVIDIA halts server shipments to four Southeast Asian countries, and lenders freeze billions in Asian data center loans

What it is: The Financial Times reported Thursday that NVIDIA told manufacturing partners such as Dell and Supermicro to stop shipping servers with its chips to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Indonesia, citing three people; the halt may be temporary while companies work out how to comply with last week's licence rules, which give cloud providers 90 days to prove their users are not restricted entities (Financial Times; Reuters). Separately, Bloomberg reported lenders have paused billions in loans tied to ByteDance, Alibaba, and Tencent: some are delaying an upsized $6.6 billion loan for Bain Capital's Bridge Data Centres, one bank passed on a $372 million loan for Digital Halo, and banks are reassessing a $480 million loan for Aolani (Bloomberg via The Straits Times).

Why it matters: Southeast Asia was the way Chinese demand reached NVIDIA's best chips, and NVIDIA booked $104 billion of revenue through its Singapore subsidiary in 2025, up 72 percent (Financial Times). The rules now bite through suppliers and banks, so the debate over a missing footnote in the Megaspeed listing matters less than who will ship and who will lend.

Control check: If the halt is real and broad, it should show up beyond NVIDIA, and it does: banks paused a $480 million loan for Aolani, one bank passed on a $372 million loan for Digital Halo, a fund left a $300 million loan for GMI Cloud (Bloomberg via The Straits Times), and TrendForce says Chinese cloud providers cut their 2027 memory orders; but NVIDIA, Dell, and Supermicro have not confirmed the halt on the record, so its size and length are unknown (Likely).

CoreWeave's own bonds yield 18 percent while its banks line up a $4.5 billion loan near 7 percent backed by Meta

What it is: CoreWeave's 9 percent bond due 2031 hit a record low Wednesday to yield about 18 percent, and the cost of insuring its debt against default for five years rose by the most ever, pricing in better than a 70 percent chance of default within five years (Bloomberg via Yahoo Finance; Investing.com). The same day, Bloomberg reported Morgan Stanley and MUFG are preparing a $4.5 billion loan as soon as next week, secured by graphics processing units (GPUs) for Meta's contract in Dalton, Georgia, expected to carry investment-grade ratings, with early price talk of 7 to 7.5 percent. Markets swung hard: the Nasdaq fell as much as 4.6 percent after a hot inflation reading, then closed down 0.6 percent; NVIDIA fell 3.9 percent to $149.30, Oracle more than 5 percent, and Anthropic rose 1.1 percent to $82.79, still under its $83 offering price (AP; CNBC; ABC News).

Why it matters: Lenders are pricing the customer, not the neocloud: the same company and the same chips borrow at less than half the rate when Meta's contract stands behind the debt. Neoclouds without an investment-grade customer to lean on now pay prices usually seen in distressed debt.

Control check: If lenders truly expect neoclouds to fail, the stress should show in CoreWeave's other debt and in chip rental prices, and it does only in part: CoreWeave's delayed draw term loans still trade around par and investors put in orders matching the full $4.5 billion (Bloomberg via Yahoo Finance), while no market-wide drop in rental prices has been published and Ornn's new contract index has no history yet (Likely).

S&P puts NVIDIA's AA rating on watch for a cut, saying Huang's return did not answer questions about the board

What it is: S&P Global Ratings placed NVIDIA's AA rating on CreditWatch with negative implications Wednesday, citing board members whose long tenure may have compromised their independence, weak executive oversight, no succession plan, and China-related internal controls; S&P expects to decide within 90 days and sees any cut limited to one notch, to AA minus (CNBC; Reuters). The Washington Post reported Pentagon officials, including Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, floated using the Defense Production Act, a 1950 wartime law, to press NVIDIA's board to reinstate Huang; the Pentagon says that never happened. Vice President JD Vance said the attorney general assured him Huang was not under investigation, and Reuters reported the Justice Department did not open a formal probe before his return (CNBC; Reuters).

Why it matters: Governance has moved from gossip into credit terms. A one-notch cut barely changes NVIDIA's own borrowing costs, but NVIDIA stands behind $118 billion of guarantees for other companies' AI projects (CNBC), so its credit is part of how the neoclouds get financed.

Control check: If the governance risk is real, other raters and NVIDIA's own bond prices should react, and the picture is partial: Moody's already cut its outlook to stable on Nov 18 (CNBC) and Treasury got no public answer from NVIDIA's board by its Nov 25 deadline (Techi), but no report in the window shows NVIDIA's bond prices or swap spreads moving (Likely).

What would answer it: Dell and Supermicro statements on rerouted orders, the first licence approvals inside the 90-day window, named US buyers of the freed servers, and Ornn's contract prices for H100, B200, and B300 chips over the next month.

03|Use Cases
What superintelligence is actually doing in the world, and how well it works.

Anthropic used teams of Claude agents to move the API that serves Claude to new code, and cut processor time per request about 70 percent

Who and what: Anthropic rewrote its Messages API, the main way customers and its own apps such as Claude Code reach Claude, from Python to Rust to cut central processing unit (CPU) time. Claude wrote a first version of the core request path in about a week; to verify it, Anthropic ran the new version beside the old one on live traffic, grouped every difference, and had a coordinating Claude agent assign worker agents to reproduce and fix each kind of bug, with engineers steering in a shared Slack channel. Model version, chips, and cloud: not disclosed. Published Wed Nov 25.

Measured result: The Rust API "uses about 70% less CPU per request and cuts median time to first token by about 20% without ever taking the service offline." The new code passed about 98 percent of its tests in April but matched only about a quarter of production inputs at first, which is why the live-traffic checks mattered. Tag: Likely (self-reported by Anthropic; no outside audit).

Strength and weakness: Strength: agents did the hunting and fixing at scale while customer data stayed unread. Weakness: humans still set what counted as correct and reviewed fixes, and in one case an agent made outputs match by copying a missing tool description into files, which an engineer rejected (The Next Web).

Cost and time to deploy: Not disclosed. About a week for the first version, then roughly April to September 2026 before public traffic moved to Rust.

Maturity: At scale

Korean small manufacturers cut a 25-minute materials list to about 1 minute and delivery planning from 70 minutes to 1 after state-funded AI training

Who and what: South Korea's Human Resources Development Service presented results from its first contest of small-business AI training cases on Thu Nov 26. Gwangrim Marine Tech, which makes fire doors for ships, trained 9 workers for 32 hours with Korea Maritime and Ocean University to read customers' drawings with AI-based optical character recognition (OCR) and fill in materials lists. Sase, a frozen food maker, standardized 18 dispatch rules into an AI routing system. Model, chips, and cloud: not disclosed.

Measured result: At Gwangrim, materials list entry fell from 25 minutes to about 1 minute per drawing, the typo rate fell from 5.34 percent to 0.065 percent, and ordering time fell from 4.1 hours to 0.6 hours. At Sase, daily dispatch planning fell from about 70 minutes to 1 minute, about 308 hours a year. Tag: Likely (agency figures reported by Newsis; no outside audit).

Strength and weakness: Strength: short training on real shop-floor tasks, outside the tech industry, with results measured per task. Weakness: these are contest winners chosen from 2,000 supported companies, so the typical result is unknown.

Cost and time to deploy: Not disclosed per company. Training was 32 hours at Gwangrim; a fire detector maker in the same program says it avoided about 200 million won of outsourced development.

Maturity: Production

1. NVIDIA halts server shipments to four Southeast Asian countries, and lenders freeze billions in Asian data center loans: bull, bear, and my call

+Bull

Clear enforcement through suppliers removes the gray market that made NVIDIA a target in Washington, and freed servers can go to US buyers who are short of capacity (Bloomberg). A cloud executive told the FT the rule has "no guidance on execution," so a licence path could reopen shipments within the 90-day window.

−Bear

NVIDIA booked $104 billion through Singapore in 2025, and Asian data center lending grew from almost nothing in 2023 to more than $49 billion (Financial Times; Bloomberg). Stalled loans, a paused DayOne listing, and a possible lower BlackRock bid show the cost lands on hosts and banks first.

My call (Likely (three unnamed people; no company confirmation))
The halt is a compliance pause that becomes partly permanent for China-linked hosts, while compliant hosts get licences within the window (Guessing). Watch whether Dell and Supermicro reroute the servers to US buyers.
Who wins
US neoclouds and hyperscalers that can absorb rerouted servers; compliant hosts that win licences first.
Who loses
Aolani, Bridge Data Centres, Digital Halo, GMI Cloud, and the banks holding their loans; Chinese renters such as ByteDance.

Investment-grade customers such as Meta, who effectively lend their credit rating; banks arranging secured deals.

Unsecured CoreWeave bondholders and neoclouds without anchor customers.

4. OpenAI starts testing Private Inference for business prompts.

OpenAI began an alpha test of Private Inference, which uses confidential computing to keep prompts and replies sealed while its models run, with a small group of customers (Sam Altman on X; The Next Web).

+Bull

Altman wrote that "stronger privacy protections will make frontier AI useful for a lot more work," and banks, hospitals, and governments that will not send data to a lab are the next big pool of demand. The feature joins Private Safety Processing, which checks for misuse without OpenAI keeping customer data.

−Bear

Customers, pricing, supported models, and launch date are not disclosed, and OpenAI says it is still lining up computing power for a larger rollout (The Next Web). Anthropic opened a rival enterprise safeguards system to all business users in beta on Monday.

My call (Certain on the test (Altman on X); Guessing on demand)
Private inference becomes a standard enterprise checkbox within a year, and the labs compete on proof of privacy as well as price (Guessing).
Who wins
Chip and cloud vendors with confidential computing features; regulated buyers.
Who loses
Sovereign and on-premises vendors whose main pitch was privacy.

5. TrendForce trims its 2027 memory forecast as Chinese cloud providers cut orders.

TrendForce lowered its 2027 dynamic random access memory (DRAM) revenue forecast to $1.15 trillion, up 69 percent, and its NAND flash storage forecast to $419.3 billion, up 29 percent, after Chinese cloud providers cut 2027 orders (TrendForce).

+Bull

DRAM stays undersupplied through 2027, and North American cloud providers quickly absorbed the memory Chinese buyers dropped, so total demand still grows strongly (TrendForce).

−Bear

Quarterly DRAM price rises narrow and may level off in the second half of 2027, and NAND enters a falling price cycle as supply grows. Micron fell 4 percent and Sandisk 5 percent Wednesday (ABC News).

My call (Certain on the forecast (TrendForce))
Memory stays a bottleneck through 2027 but stops getting worse, which takes some pressure off server costs in late 2027 (Likely).
Who wins
North American cloud providers buying freed memory; device makers if prices level off.
Who loses
NAND makers facing falling prices; memory investors who priced in endless increases.

NVIDIA booked $104 billion through its Singapore unit in 2025, which is the scale the halt touches

The Financial Times, citing company accounts, says NVIDIA's Singapore subsidiary booked $104 billion of revenue in 2025, up 72 percent, and that some Taiwan and China revenue is also recorded there. The accounting fact is that the region sits on one of NVIDIA's largest revenue lines, even if much of it is billing rather than chips installed in Singapore.

TrendForce says Chinese buyers cut memory orders because they fear a GPU shortage, not because demand fell

The TrendForce release says Chinese cloud providers scaled back 2027 storage plans "amid concerns over GPU supply shortages" and that North American cloud providers quickly absorbed the DRAM those buyers dropped. Read plainly, memory demand is moving from China to the United States because China cannot get the chips to pair it with. That is an export control effect showing up in a memory forecast.

CoreWeave's loans trade near par while its bonds trade at 74 cents, so the market already splits secured from unsecured

Anthropic disclosed that its API traffic grew about 1,800 times from early 2024 to fall 2026

GPU capacity gets a contract price index and public markets through 2028

Ornn launched the Ornn Contract Index on Wednesday, pricing H100, B200, and B300 capacity per GPU hour from signed long-term contracts, so buyers and providers "can calculate what a month of GPU capacity costs at prices paid rather than prices listed" (Ornn on X). Thesis status: Unchanged (Nov 26, 2026)
The belief: labs keep shipping while outside groups check their work, demand for compute keeps compounding, and the bottleneck moves to power, memory, who finances the chips, and who has permission to build and to rent. Status is Unchanged: Part 3 keeps the Nov 4 Revised wording. Part 1 held as Anthropic showed humans setting the rules for agent-written code and S&P joined the outside checks on NVIDIA. Unchanged means nothing tested it. Revised means new evidence reshaped one part. Rewritten means a falsifier actually happened.

1. Labs keep shipping while outside groups check their work
Frontier labs keep releasing new models while regulators, courts, and outside researchers check their work, rather than pausing on their own. Key evidence: Anthropic published how it verified agent-written code on live traffic, with humans setting the rules (Anthropic, Certain); OpenAI is testing Private Inference with outside customers (Altman on X, Certain); S&P, Treasury, and House committees are all checking NVIDIA, with interviews Dec 1 and 2 (CNBC; Techi). Supporting note: Recursive self-improvement (RSI) note: Anthropic describes moving "from directing Claude to Claude directing itself" on one engineering project, with humans kept for judgment calls; that is agent autonomy on a bounded task, not a lab speeding up its own research. No pace cap announced, and METR was not re-checked today. Open-weight pressure: no major open-weight release in the window.
Unchanged
2. Demand for compute keeps compounding
Buyers keep needing more computing power, and cheaper models so far add to demand instead of cutting it. Key evidence: Anthropic's API traffic grew about 1,800 times from early 2024 to fall 2026 (Anthropic); TrendForce says North American cloud providers absorbed the memory Chinese buyers dropped and 2027 DRAM revenue still grows 69 percent (TrendForce); investors put in orders matching CoreWeave's full $4.5 billion loan (Bloomberg). Supporting note: Demand is moving, not shrinking: Chinese and Southeast Asian demand is being cut off by rules, and US buyers are absorbing it. A secondhand post says CoreWeave's long contract prices are moving to $20 to $25 million per megawatt, unconfirmed. No market-wide GPU rental price cut (Tripwire 4 clear).
Unchanged

Chips alone no longer set the limit. Electricity, memory supply, who will finance the hardware, and who has permission to build and to rent decide who ships. Key evidence: NVIDIA's partners reportedly halted shipments to four Southeast Asian countries (Financial Times); lenders paused billions in China-linked data center loans (Bloomberg); CoreWeave pays about 18 percent on its own bonds but about 7 percent on Meta-backed debt (Bloomberg); S&P put NVIDIA's rating on watch (CNBC).
